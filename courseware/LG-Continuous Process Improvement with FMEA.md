# Topic 1 — Introduction to Continuous Process Improvement

**Alignment:** ELO1 · A1 · A2 · K1  
**Focus:** Culture · governance · PDCA · process definition

## Topic Orientation

### System, not event

Continuous improvement is a managed cycle of evidence, action, learning and standardisation.

### People closest to work

Operators and technicians surface weak signals and practical constraints.

### PDCA discipline

Test a defined change, compare evidence and standardise only what works.

### Shared process view

SIPOC and a process map establish boundaries before analysis begins.

## Concept Guide

### Improvement is a management system

A workshop creates ideas; a system creates repeatable learning, ownership and control.

### What continuous improvement changes

Performance: Reduce defects, delay, cost and instability against a defined baseline.

Capability: Build reliable processes that repeatedly meet customer and regulatory needs.

Learning: Turn experiments, failures and operator knowledge into standard work.

Culture: Make problem visibility safe and improvement part of normal work.

### Continuous improvement and breakthrough change

First perspective: Frequent, evidence-led adjustments; Lower implementation risk; Builds habits and process knowledge.

Contrast or design check: Larger redesign or technology shift; Higher uncertainty and coordination; May be necessary when the current system is structurally incapable.

### Lean, Six Sigma and FMEA

Lean: Improve flow; Remove non-value work; Shorten lead time.

Six Sigma: Reduce variation; Use data to test causes; Improve capability.

FMEA: Anticipate failure; Prioritise prevention; Link risk to controls.

### A culture that can improve

Will: Leaders make priorities and trade-offs explicit.

Skill: Teams know how to frame, analyse, test and control.

Authority: Owners can act within agreed boundaries.

Guidance: Coaching and standards turn tools into good judgement.

### Continuous improvement governance

Sponsor sets direction → Process owner defines gap → Cross-functional team analyses → Action owners implement → Governance reviews evidence

### PDCA turns change into learning

Plan: define gap, target and hypothesis → Do: test at controlled scale → Check: compare evidence and side effects → Act: standardise, adjust or abandon

### Plan with evidence

Problem: Current versus required performance, with scope and time.

Baseline: Trusted operational data and agreed definitions.

Target: Specific outcome, measure and time horizon.

Hypothesis: Why the proposed countermeasure should change the result.

### Team roles in improvement

Sponsor: Removes barriers and protects the business priority.

Process owner: Owns performance and the post-project control system.

Facilitator: Keeps the method rigorous and participation balanced.

Operators and experts: Provide actual-work knowledge and test feasibility.

Data owner: Protects definitions, lineage and evidence quality.

Action owner: Implements, verifies and documents a countermeasure.

### SIPOC establishes the analysis boundary

Suppliers → Inputs → Process → Outputs → Customers

### Voice of customer and voice of process

First perspective: Needs, complaints and field effects; Critical-to-quality expectations; What failure means to the user.

Contrast or design check: Yield, cycle time and defect mechanisms; Variation, controls and capability; What the operation can repeatedly deliver.

### Coaching for team support

Ask before telling: Use questions to surface assumptions and evidence.

Make work visible: Use boards, owners and dates so risk is shared.

Reinforce learning: Review what changed and why, not only the result.

Protect candour: Treat an exposed weakness as useful information.

# Topic 2 — Root Cause Analysis

**Alignment:** ELO2 · A3 · A4  
**Focus:** Evidence · Pareto · Fishbone · 5 Whys · Fault Tree

## Topic Orientation

### Describe the gap

A good problem statement names the measure, location, timing and magnitude without guessing the cause.

### Separate cause from symptom

The customer effect, process failure and causal mechanism belong at different levels.

### Triangulate

Use data prioritisation, structured brainstorming and verification together.

### Mechanism over blame

Useful causes describe how the process creates failure and point toward controllable action.

## Concept Guide

### A root cause is a verified mechanism

It is not the first plausible explanation, a correlation, or the person nearest the failure.

### Build a neutral problem statement

What: The measurable defect, delay or performance gap.

Where: Process step, line, product family or customer segment.

When: Start, pattern, shift, period or change point.

How much: Magnitude, rate, trend and business/customer impact.

### Cause, failure and consequence

First perspective: Cause: stencil aperture partly blocked; Mechanism: insufficient paste transfer; Controllable through cleaning and verification.

Contrast or design check: Failure mode: insufficient solder joint; Effect: intermittent signal or field return; Observed symptom is not automatically the root cause.

### Pareto focuses the investigation

Illustrative defect mix: Solder bridge 42, Insufficient solder 26, Misalignment 15, Wrong component 9, Tombstone 5, Other 3. The categories sum to 100; use the cumulative percentage to focus investigation without claiming the largest category is a root cause.

### Read a Pareto chart correctly

Rank: Bars order categories by frequency or impact.

Cumulative line: Shows how quickly the categories account for the total.

Stratify: Check shift, product and machine before combining data.

Do not overclaim: The largest category is a priority, not yet a cause.

### Fishbone structures possible causes

People → Machine → Method → Material → Measurement → Environment

### From brainstorm to evidence

Candidate cause: Write a specific mechanism, not a vague label.

Prediction: State what data pattern would be expected if it were true.

Test: Compare lots, shifts, machines, materials or controlled trials.

Decision: Confirm, reject or keep open with the next evidence needed.

### Five Whys follows one causal chain

Observed failure → Immediate mechanism → Process condition → System weakness → Controllable root cause

### Strong and weak 5 Whys

First perspective: Each answer is evidence-supported; The chain stays on one mechanism; The endpoint suggests a system change.

Contrast or design check: Jumps between unrelated branches; Ends with 'operator error'; Treats five as a magic number.

### Fault Tree Analysis tests combinations

Top event → OR: any branch can cause it → AND: conditions must combine → Basic events with evidence

### Fishbone, 5 Whys and Fault Tree

First perspective: Fishbone widens the search; 5 Whys deepens one likely chain; Use when mechanisms are still being explored.

Contrast or design check: Fault Tree formalises logical combinations; Supports safety and reliability reasoning; Use when several paths can create the top event.

### Data quality checks before conclusion

Definition: Are defect categories and denominators consistent?

Measurement: Can inspectors or sensors reproduce the classification?

Completeness: Are rework, scrap and escaped defects all represented?

Context: Are product mix, shift and process changes recorded?

# Topic 3 — Design and Apply FMEA

**Alignment:** ELO3 · A5 · A6 · K3  
**Focus:** Scope · function · failure chain · risk · action · learning

## Topic Orientation

### Prospective thinking

FMEA asks what could fail before the next customer or process loss occurs.

### Failure chain

Function → failure mode → effect → cause → controls makes risk traceable.

### Risk-informed action

Severity, occurrence and detection guide judgement; RPN alone never grants permission to ignore risk.

### Living knowledge

Actions, verification, re-rating and review triggers keep the FMEA current.

## Concept Guide

### FMEA is prevention made visible

It documents what might fail, why it matters, what controls exist and who will reduce the risk.

**Source note:** ASQ defines FMEA as a systematic, proactive method for evaluating where and how a process might fail and the relative impact of those failures.

### When FMEA has the most leverage

New design or process: Prevent known patterns before approval or launch.

Changed use: Reassess risk when a product or process enters a new context.

Improvement goal: Anticipate failure introduced by the proposed change.

Before Control Plan: Convert prioritised risks into prevention and detection controls.

### Three common FMEA views

System FMEA: Interfaces and interactions; System functions; Cross-boundary effects.

Design FMEA: Product functions; Materials and geometry; Design prevention.

Process FMEA: How production introduces defects; 6M causes; Feeds the Control Plan.

### Prospective FMEA and retrospective RCA

First perspective: Asks: what could fail?; Acts before the next loss; Uses functions, experience and change knowledge.

Contrast or design check: Asks: what happened and why?; Acts after a failure signal; Verified findings should update the FMEA.

**Source note:** FMEA anticipates credible future failure; RCA explains evidence from an observed event. Verified RCA learning must update the FMEA.

### Seven-step FMEA method

Plan & prepare → Structure → Function → Failure → Risk → Optimise → Document results

**Source note:** This guide uses the seven-step planning, structure, function, failure, risk, optimisation and documentation sequence described in current AIAG/VDA-style practice.

### Step 1 — planning and preparation

Purpose: Name the decision the FMEA must support.

Scope: Set product/process boundaries and interfaces.

Team: Include design, process, quality, maintenance and operators.

Inputs: Gather flow maps, complaints, controls, changes and lessons.

### Steps 2–3 — structure and function

Structure: Break the process into logical steps and focus elements.

Interfaces: Show upstream, downstream and support interactions.

Function: Use verb–noun language with a measurable requirement.

Requirement: State the expected output, tolerance or customer need.

### Step 4 — build the failure chain

Function → Failure mode → Effect → Cause → Current controls

### Write useful failure statements

Failure mode: Loss, partial, intermittent, excessive or unintended function.

Effect: What the next operation, system or customer experiences.

Cause: A specific mechanism or process condition that creates the mode.

Control: What prevents the cause or detects cause/mode before escape.

### Prevention and detection controls

First perspective: Remove or reduce the cause; Standard work, robust design, interlock; Influences occurrence.

Contrast or design check: Reveal cause or failure before escape; Sensor, test, inspection, alarm; Influences detection ranking.

### Step 5 — S, O and D describe different risk dimensions

Severity: consequence → Occurrence: likelihood → Detection: ability to catch before impact

### Severity rating discipline

Customer effect: Rate the most serious credible effect for the failure mode.

Consistent scale: Use one agreed 1–10 table and record the rationale.

Safety/regulatory: Escalate high-severity concerns regardless of a modest RPN.

Design change: Detection alone does not reduce the consequence severity.

### Occurrence rating discipline

Cause-specific: Rate the likelihood of the stated causal mechanism.

Use evidence: Prefer capability, defect, maintenance or comparable-history data.

Prevention matters: Strong prevention can reduce occurrence.

Assumptions visible: Record uncertainty and the basis for the score.

### Detection rating discipline

Higher means weaker: A high D score means the failure is hard to detect.

Control-specific: Rate the actual detection control, not intended inspection.

Before effect: Ask whether the cause or mode is caught before customer impact.

Evidence: Use validation, gauge reliability and escape history.

### RPN is useful—but incomplete

Calculate: RPN = S × O × D; Range 1–1000; Useful for sorting and trending.

Interpret: Ordinal ratings; Same RPN can hide different profiles; Record scoring rationale.

Guardrail: No fixed cutoff as permission; Severity first; Use team judgement and Action Priority.

**Source note:** Severity, occurrence and detection are ordinal judgements. Similar RPN values can represent very different risk profiles, so severity and action priority remain visible.

### Same number, different risk

First perspective: S=10, O=2, D=2; RPN = 40; Potentially safety-critical effect.

Contrast or design check: S=5, O=4, D=2; RPN = 40; Different consequence and action logic.

### Step 6 — optimise in the right order

Eliminate or reduce the effect → Prevent the cause / reduce occurrence → Improve detection → Assign owner and due date → Verify then re-rate

### Strong recommended actions

Specific: Names the change, location and failure mechanism.

Owned: One accountable owner and a realistic due date.

Verifiable: Defines evidence that proves implementation and effectiveness.

Traceable: Links to the PFMEA row, change record and Control Plan.

### Step 7 — document and sustain

Record decisions → Confirm actions → Re-score verified state → Update Control Plan → Set review triggers

### Review triggers for a living FMEA

Engineering change: New material, equipment, tooling, software or parameter.

Process signal: Capability loss, special cause, defect trend or downtime.

Customer signal: Complaint, return, field failure or changed requirement.

Learning: RCA, audit, maintenance finding or supplier feedback.

### FMEA pitfalls

Scope creep: A boundary too broad prevents meaningful depth.

Template completion: Scoring without insight or action creates false assurance.

RPN gaming: Adjusting ratings to cross a threshold hides risk.

Missing operators: The team loses actual-work failure knowledge.

No ownership: Actions without owner/date remain recommendations.

Stale document: A frozen FMEA stops reflecting the real process.

# Topic 4 — Identify and Direct Improvement Opportunities

**Alignment:** ELO4 · A7 · A8 · K2  
**Focus:** Prioritise · select · pilot · coach · implement

## Topic Orientation

### Target the failure mechanism

Prevention is stronger than inspection when the cause can be removed or constrained.

### Choose transparently

Decision criteria make trade-offs visible and reduce preference-driven selection.

### Pilot before scale

A bounded test checks benefit, side effects, capability and adoption.

### Direct with ownership

Every action needs an owner, due date, success measure and escalation path.

## Concept Guide

### The best action changes the failure mechanism

Inspection may catch a defect; prevention changes why it occurs.

### From risk to improvement portfolio

High-priority risk → Countermeasure options → Decision criteria → Pilot and evidence → Approved implementation

### Countermeasure hierarchy

Prevent: Eliminate the cause; Robust design; Poka-yoke or interlock.

Reduce: Improve capability; Control variation; Standard work and maintenance.

Detect: Automated checks; Validated inspection; Fast containment signal.

### Opportunity sources

PFMEA: High severity, Action Priority, occurrence and weak detection.

Pareto and RCA: Recurring defects and verified mechanisms.

Capability and SPC: Unstable or incapable critical characteristics.

People and customers: Operator constraints, complaints and near misses.

### DMAIC provides a project roadmap

Define: Clarify the customer, problem, scope, target and ownership.

Measure: Validate definitions and establish a trustworthy baseline.

Analyse: Prioritise patterns and verify causal mechanisms.

Improve: Select, pilot and implement countermeasures.

Control: Monitor, react, standardise and transfer ownership.

Relationship to PDCA: Both are evidence-led cycles; DMAIC gives a detailed improvement-project structure.

### 5S supports stable process conditions

Sort: Remove items that are not needed for the work.

Set in order: Make the correct item and sequence obvious.

Shine: Clean while inspecting for early abnormality.

Standardise: Define a repeatable condition and visual method.

Sustain: Audit, coach and correct drift as normal work.

### DOE tests factors and interactions

First perspective: Plan combinations of factor levels; Estimate main effects and interactions; Use randomisation, replication and blocking where appropriate.

Contrast or design check: Do not change many factors informally; Confirm measurement and process stability first; Use statistical expertise for safety-critical or complex designs.

### Evaluation criteria

Risk reduction: Expected change to severity, occurrence or detection.

Effectiveness: Strength of evidence and fit to the causal mechanism.

Feasibility: Skills, time, technology and operational constraints.

Cost and speed: Investment, lifecycle cost and time to benefit.

Side effects: New failure modes, workload or compliance concerns.

Sustainability: Ability to standardise, train, monitor and maintain.

### Weighted criteria matrix and Pugh matrix

First perspective: Scores options against weighted criteria; Useful when priorities differ in importance; Shows a total and sensitivity to weights.

Contrast or design check: Compares each option with a datum; Uses better / same / worse reasoning; Useful for concept screening and discussion.

### Cost-benefit without false precision

Benefit: Avoided scrap, rework, downtime, complaints and risk exposure.

Cost: Capital, validation, training, maintenance and transition loss.

Time: Separate one-off savings from recurring annual impact.

Uncertainty: Show assumptions and ranges instead of one confident number.

### Pilot learning cycle

Hypothesis and success measures → Safe scope and baseline → Run with controls → Compare result and side effects → Decide scale / revise / stop

### A credible pilot

Representative: Covers the relevant product, shift and failure conditions.

Contained: Limits customer and production risk during the test.

Measured: Uses defined input, process and outcome measures.

Reversible: Includes a stop rule and recovery plan.

### Poka-yoke strategies

Contact: Physical fit or sensor; Detects shape/position; Prevents wrong assembly.

Fixed value: Counts required items or actions; Signals omissions; Useful for kits and fasteners.

Motion sequence: Enforces task order; Interlock or checklist; Prevents skipped steps.

### Direct and coach the team

Purpose: Explain the customer/risk reason for the change.

Practice: Demonstrate, observe and let the operator perform.

Feedback: Correct the method and invite actual-work concerns.

Follow-up: Check adoption, outcome and unintended effects.

### Implementation control

Owner and date: One accountable person and milestone.

Change control: Approved specification, work instruction and training.

Verification: Evidence the action was installed as intended.

Effectiveness: Evidence it reduced risk without creating new failure.

# Topic 5 — Manage Improvement with a Process Control Plan

**Alignment:** ELO5 · A9 · A10 · K4  
**Focus:** Variation · capability · SPC · reaction · review

## Topic Orientation

### Control the critical few

Translate high-risk PFMEA causes and characteristics into practical controls.

### Know the variation

Common-cause variation needs system improvement; special causes need investigation.

### React by design

A Control Plan must state who acts, what is contained and how the process restarts.

### Close the loop

Control results, incidents and changes feed back into the PFMEA and standard work.

## Concept Guide

### Control is a feedback system

A stable process detects meaningful change, contains risk and learns before the customer does.

### Common-cause and special-cause variation

First perspective: Built into the current system; Many small sources; Improve the process as a system.

Contrast or design check: Specific, unusual signal; Assignable event or condition; Investigate and remove the cause.

### Stability and capability answer different questions

Stability: Is the process behaviour predictable over time?

Capability: Can a stable process meet specification consistently?

Sequence: Check stability before interpreting capability indices.

Customer: Specifications come from requirements; control limits come from process data.

### Cp and Cpk

Cp: Potential capability; Specification width ÷ 6σ; Assumes centring.

Cpk: Actual centring-aware capability; Minimum distance to limit ÷ 3σ; Cpk ≤ Cp.

Interpret: Use stable data; Confirm distribution and measurement; Report assumptions.

### A control chart separates signal from noise

Plot observations in time order, calculate a centre line and statistically derived control limits, then investigate non-random signals. Control limits describe observed behaviour; specification limits describe requirements.

### Control limits and specification limits

First perspective: Calculated from process behaviour; Support stability decisions; Not changed to make the chart look better.

Contrast or design check: Defined by customer/design requirement; Support conformance decisions; Do not describe natural process variation.

### Control-chart signals

Point beyond a limit: Immediate special-cause investigation.

Run on one side: Possible process shift.

Trend: Gradual change, wear or drift.

Cycle or clustering: Time pattern, batching or stratification.

### The Control Plan operationalises the PFMEA

Process step / characteristic → Specification and method → Sample size and frequency → Owner and record → Reaction plan

### Control selection principles

Risk-based: Strength and frequency reflect severity, occurrence and escape risk.

At source: Control as close as possible to the causal process condition.

Capable measurement: The method can reliably distinguish acceptable from unacceptable.

Actionable: A signal must trigger a defined response, not only a record.

### Reaction plan

Stop or contain → Identify affected product → Notify owner → Correct and verify → Release and update records

### Monitoring and evaluation

First perspective: Leading: parameter adherence, preventive maintenance, alarm rate; Detect drift before outcome loss; Useful for early intervention.

Contrast or design check: Lagging: defect, scrap, rework, return, downtime; Confirms realised outcome; Useful for accountability and learning.

### Evaluate an improvement

Effectiveness: Did the targeted measure improve against baseline?

Sustainment: Did the result persist across time, shift and product mix?

Side effects: Did another defect, delay or workload appear?

Adoption: Is standard work followed and understood?

Risk: Are PFMEA ratings and controls updated with evidence?

Learning: What should be standardised, transferred or investigated next?

### Closed-loop improvement

Monitor control data → Detect signal → Contain and investigate → Update PFMEA and action → Revise Control Plan and standard work

### Governance review questions

Right risk?: Are high-severity and high-priority items visible?

Right evidence?: Are ratings, capability and results based on trusted data?

Right action?: Does the countermeasure address the verified mechanism?

Right control?: Will drift be detected and contained in time?
